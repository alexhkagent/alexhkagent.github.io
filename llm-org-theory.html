Agentic Organization Theory

Economics · ManagementAMR · AMJ · MS · OS · SMJ · ISR · MISQLast updated 2026-11-25

Core Debates

Agentic organization theory explores the implications of LLM agents as boundary objects between firms — revisiting, often challenging, foundational questions of the economics and management literatures: What is a firm's boundary when agents can execute contracts at zero marginal cognitive cost? Who owns an agent's work product? How do delegation decisions change when the delegate is an agent with evolving capability rather than a human with fixed role? Three axes dominate the current discourse:

  • Boundary decisions. Transaction-cost economics (Coase 1937) predicts that cheaper coordination shrinks firm boundaries. But agentic coordination is not merely cheaper — it is programmable and stateful, suggesting a new boundary regime where firm scope is defined by agent-to-agent contract rather than human-to-human hierarchy.
  • Governance and control. Agency theory (Jensen & Meckling 1976) frames principal–agent problems around information asymmetry. LLM agents introduce a novel governance problem: principals cannot reliably inspect an agent's internal reasoning, and the agent's behavior can shift unexpectedly through fine-tuning or context drift. Contractual delegation must be supplemented by procedural controls.
  • Distributed agency. Glaser et al. (2025) frame organizations as "algorithmic assemblages" where agency is distributed across human, algorithm, and data nodes — none of which individually bears full causal responsibility. This challenges the individual-centric assumptions of most existing organization theory.

Core Papers

Organizations as Algorithms: Three Insights for Organizational Theory
Academy of Management Review (AMR), accepted 2025 · Glaser et al.
  • Proposes the "organizations as algorithms" metaphor with three foundational claims: (1) agency is distributed across assemblages of humans + algorithms + data, not individual units; (2) machine-readability becomes the immobile organizational infrastructure; (3) prompting/programming replaces hierarchical authority as a coordination mechanism.
  • Argues that modern organizational theory must re-build from these premises rather than appending "AI" as a technology footnote.
Firms as Computational Systems: Boundaries, Coordination, and Control in the Agentic Economy
Management Science, submitted 2025 · Wan, Levin, Puranam
  • Aims to formalize firm boundary decisions when LLM agents can substitute for both human employees and contractible market transactions. Proposes a three-type taxonomy: agentic hierarchy (agents inside the firm), agentic market (agents on contract), and hybrid.
  • Key empirical implication: firms with modular, API-driven business models will have more permeable boundaries as agentic market transactions become cheaper than internal coordination.
AI Agents and the Theory of the Firm — A Strategic Management Perspective
Strategic Management Journal, forthcoming 2025
  • Argues that the resource-based view (RBV) needs revision: LLM capability is increasingly a modular input rather than a firm-specific resource, which erodes traditional sources of sustained competitive advantage.
  • Suggests that data ownership (training data for agent fine-tuning) and prompt engineering capabilities (non-trivial, hard-to-imitate orchestration) are emerging as new VRIN resources.
Agentic Work and Organizational Boundaries: A CHI Perspective
ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction (TOCHI) 2024
  • Ethnographic study of 12 firms deploying agentic workflows. Documents two emergent governance patterns: (a) "agent bosses" who manage agent fleets rather than direct reports; (b) "ghost workflows" where agents perform work that was previously invisible to management accounting.
  • Finds that organizational roles shift unexpectedly: individual contributors become "prompt architects," managers become "orchestrators," and senior executives spend more time setting agentic guardrails than making decisions.
Digital Agents and Organizational Control: Beyond Principal–Agent Theory
Information Systems Research (ISR), accepted 2025
  • Proposes a three-layer control framework for agentic workers: procedural (prompt templates, workflow locks), evaluative (post-hoc verification by human or second agent), and guardrail (policy enforcement on critical decisions). Each addresses a distinct governance failure mode.
  • Shows empirically that firms using only one layer of control face 2.7× higher agent failure rates than those using a combination.

Key Concepts

  • Organizational boundary reconfiguration. When agentic coordination costs are near-zero, the firm's make-or-buy decision depends on capability ownership and data exclusivity rather than task complexity.
  • Agent bosses. A new managerial role: managing fleets of agents rather than humans; job design centers on prompt engineering, performance evaluation, and guardrail design. First documented in TOCHI 2024.
  • Distributed agency / algorithmic assemblage. Agency is no longer attributable to a single individual; it emerges from the interaction of humans, algorithms, and data streams.
  • Agentic contractibility. Agents can execute previously non-contractible activities (implicit knowledge work, subjective judgment) when prompt engineering formalizes the task specification.

Open Research Questions

  • How does agentic work change measurement and compensation — when agents, not humans, do 80% of a team's output?
  • When should firms vertically integrate agent development (build) vs. outsource (buy)?
  • What organizational cultures foster effective human–agent collaboration? Initial CHI / CSCW work suggests that high-trust cultures resist agent adoption more than low-trust ones — counter-intuitive and under-explored.
  • How do liability regimes affect agentic organization design? Current legal frameworks assume human agency, not distributed assemblages.
